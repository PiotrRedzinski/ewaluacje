INTRODUCTION
====================================================================
Wspierająca Szkoła (Supportive School) is Poland's first comprehensive program on preventing suicidal behavior among children and adolescents, addressed to primary and secondary schools. The program's direct recipients are adults — teachers, school psychologists, pedagogues and other school staff, as well as parents. The program's goal is to give these groups relevant knowledge, improve their skills, and provide them with tools that allow them to properly care for the psychological well-being of children and youth.

The program was built on WHO (2007) guidelines on school-based prevention, which in the context of preventing suicidal behavior identify as key:
• strengthening the psychological health of adults — teachers and parents (providing informational/educational materials on psychological burden and possible mental disorders in themselves, other adults, children and youth, and ways of coping with them);
• strengthening children's and teenagers' self-esteem (emphasizing strengths and resources, showing kindness, understanding, acceptance, praise);
• educating children and teenagers on expressing and coping with their own emotions;
• preventing school violence;
• making information about help venues and helplines available.

An important reference point was also the four evidence-based suicide-prevention strategies from the WHO LIVE LIFE implementation guide (2021): developing socio-emotional competencies in children and teenagers; early identification and adequate support of people with suicidal behaviors; restricting access to lethal means; responsible media reporting of suicidal behavior.

Edition II of "Wspierająca Szkoła" was co-financed from the State budget via the National Health Program (NPZ) 2021–2025, Ministry of National Education, under the task "See the emotions – implementation of evidence-based educational, upbringing, intervention and prevention projects and programs, including universal, indicated and selective prevention programs."

This edition included four four-teaching-hour trainings for teachers:
• A Student in Psychological Crisis – How to Recognize and Support Them?
• Parents of a Student in Psychological Crisis – How to Motivate Them to Cooperate?
• A Student After a Suicide Attempt – How to Support Their Return to School?
• Teacher Psychological Well-Being – How to Cope with Stress and Difficult Emotions?

Knowledge-check surveys were prepared for each training, both before and after.

Trainers were suicidology experts who regularly provide consultations on suicidal crisis, suicide attempts, self-harm and postvention. Edition II trainers:
Jolanta Palma, Lucyna Kicińska, Małgorzata Łuba, Joanna Skiba, Magdalena Mastalerz, Maja Lipińska, Melania Dominiak, Anna Malinowska, Ewelina Cieśluk, Joanna Roch, Piotr Bakan (suicidologists, psychologists, pedagogues, crisis interventionists, psychotherapists — full credentials listed in the source document).

PART I — "A STUDENT IN PSYCHOLOGICAL CRISIS – HOW TO RECOGNIZE AND SUPPORT THEM?"
====================================================================
This training equipped participants with knowledge and tools for identifying and responding to psychological crises in children and youth: warning symptoms, long-term consequences, concrete intervention strategies with an emphasis on empathy and effectiveness, when/how to seek outside support, and building psychological resilience among youth.

1,262 people took part in this training across all 55 schools (a per-school participant table is included in the original, ranging roughly from 11 to 52 participants per school).

--- Evaluation survey ---
Format: 6 single-choice questions, 6 open questions, 3 scaled-rating questions. Combined with the post-training knowledge test, so respondent counts are identical for both. 976 people responded (~77.3% return rate).

Q: Was the way content was delivered during the meeting appropriate for you?
Yes: 965 (98.9%) | No: 11 (1.1%)

Of the 117 people who commented on possible changes (more than the 11 "No" respondents, since others chose to comment anyway), themes were:
1. No need for changes / positive rating (dominant): "Nothing needs to change," "Everything was fine," "Very satisfied," "Professionally done," etc.
2. Format preference — online vs. in-person: several wanted in-person sessions instead.
3. Duration/organization: suggestions to shorten, split into two segments, add a scheduled break, hold at an earlier time.
4. Interactivity/methods: some wanted more interaction, case exercises, more concrete reaction examples (though breakout rooms were already praised by others).
5. Minor content/language notes: discomfort with the word "clients" for therapy participants; request to keep/share slides; occasional "too general" feedback.
6. A few isolated critical opinions: "waste of time," "too much filler," "not matched to my professional experience." These were rare and non-recurring.

Q: Will the content help in your professional work? Yes: 99.1% | No: 0.9%

Open question — "Which content will be most useful in your daily work? Why?" Key themes (with representative quotes translated):
1. Talking with a student in crisis (communication, dialogue, empathy) — e.g., "How to talk with someone in a difficult situation," "how to really help instead of causing more harm," "listening rather than rushing to fix things."
2. Sample questions and ready-made phrasings — e.g., "example questions," "what to say and what not to say to a student in crisis."
3. Frameworks/tools: the FUKO method (a structured feedback/conversation protocol) and the "4Z" model (Notice–Ask–Accept–React) — frequently praised as practical and memorable.
4. Recognizing crisis and warning signs — noticing behavioral changes, non-obvious symptoms; "I didn't realize that inappropriate behavior can be a cry for help."
5. Step-by-step response — clear sequencing of actions when a crisis is disclosed.
6. Emotional first aid — step-by-step guidance for helping someone in psychological crisis.
7. High-risk topics: self-harm, suicidal thoughts/attempts — distinguishing self-harm from a suicide attempt.
8. Support pathway/procedures/collaboration ("passing the baton") — knowing when and how to hand a case to another specialist, and that doing so is legitimate.
9. Real-life examples, role-plays, case studies — praised as the most effective learning method.
10. General answers ("everything was useful," hard to pick just one).
11. A few critical/negative responses: "too many words, I got lost," "nothing new," "not relevant to my role."

Summary of this question: the most valued elements were practical conversation skills with a student in crisis — ready-made questions/phrases, the FUKO and 4Z frameworks, symptom recognition, and a clear response pathway (including emotional first aid and high-risk situations like self-harm and suicidal ideation).

Open question — "Did the meeting change how you perceive certain issues? Which ones?" Key themes:
1. Changed approach to talking/communicating with a student in crisis.
2. Empathy instead of consoling/advice-giving — realizing that reassurance, advice, and minimizing the problem are not helpful; presence and empathy matter more.
3. Distinction between empathy and sympathy/compassion.
4. "Calling things by their name" — not being afraid of difficult words, including "suicide."
5. Reframing student behavior — not labeling misbehavior, looking for underlying causes (e.g., aggression may stem from lived experience, not "bad upbringing").
6. Greater vigilance for crisis signals and better symptom recognition.
7. The "relay/baton" concept — understanding the limits of a teacher's role and the legitimacy of passing a case to a more competent person; reduced sense of sole responsibility.
8. When to call for help / emergency response (medical services, police, 112).
9. New understanding of self-harm, suicide attempts, self-destructive behavior — not "fashion," often a cry for help or a way of seeking relief.
10. Organized/consolidated existing knowledge (without a "revolution" in thinking) — many said it reinforced rather than changed what they knew.
11. No change — for those already experienced or previously trained in this area.

Summary: most often the meeting shifted participants toward empathy, active listening, and avoiding consolation/advice; many also noted greater willingness to speak plainly about difficult topics (including suicide), better understanding of student behavior (without labeling, seeking causes), and increased vigilance to crisis signs. The "relay of help" concept and clarity about when to call in outside services were also significant.

Open question — "Can you identify content you consider unnecessary in the agenda? Which?" Key themes:
1. No unnecessary content (dominant answer) — "everything was necessary," "100% informationally necessary," "every topic had practical application."
2. Content that was already known/obvious (but not seen as unnecessary) — some found parts of the material familiar from prior training, still valued as reinforcement.
3. Too much theory/definitions — suggestions to shorten theoretical parts, e.g., "neuromechanics of emotion," lengthy crisis classifications.
4. Introduction/first part too long — a handful felt the first ~30–40 minutes could be trimmed.
5. Too little practice / too much repetition — requests for more concrete examples, less theory.
6. Group-work format concerns (isolated) — a couple of participants felt breakout-group work was time-consuming without adding much.
7. Content seen as "too specialized" for teachers — some felt certain material belonged more to psychologists.
8. A couple of isolated/humorous remarks about analogies to dogs used in the training.

Open question — "Do you feel anything important was missing from the agenda? What?" Key themes:
1. "Nothing was missing" — dominant response; the topic was seen as thoroughly covered.
2. Too little time / topic too broad for 3 hours (more "could go deeper" than "something missing").
3. More practice, case studies, examples, role-plays wanted.
4. Talking with parents / difficult parent cooperation — e.g., how to inform parents, handle a parent who denies the child's crisis or is hostile.
5. What to do when a student refuses help or denies having a problem.
6. Teacher well-being / psyche — coping with the emotional burden of helping students in crisis, burnout, what to do if a student dies by suicide despite the teacher's efforts.
7. Grief after a student's death (including suicide) and how the class should process it.
8. Deeper knowledge on specific conditions — depression, PTSD, school phobia.
9. Scope of competence: teacher vs. specialist (psychologist/psychiatrist/psychotherapist).
10. The classroom as a support environment — how to prepare peers to support a student in crisis.
11. A few other isolated suggestions (e.g., dealing with a student who blames others for their problems; the topic of peer bullying/hate as an underlying stressor).

Overall meeting rating (0–10 scale): 10: 634 (65.0%) | 9: 198 (20.3%) | 8: 99 (10.1%) | 7: 23 (2.4%) | 1–6: combined <2%.

Open question — "Any additional comments?" Key themes:
1. No additional comments / full satisfaction (largest group).
2. Very high praise for the trainer's competence and delivery.
3. Appreciation for practical examples and delivery format (e.g., analogies, a case-study segment on a student in crisis, video use, breakout rooms).
4. Preference for in-person vs. online format (recurring minority view).
5. Comments on length/timing — fatigue after a full workday, 3 hours felt long.
6. Requests for even more practical content.
7. Technical/organizational notes (audio quality, connectivity, group-work logistics, double surveys taking too long).
8. Reflections on the need for support for teachers themselves.
9. Desire for continuation / more sessions.

--- Summary (Part I) ---
The evaluation of "A Student in Psychological Crisis – How to Recognize and Support Them?" shows the training was very highly rated and largely met its educational and practical goals. 976 respondents participated — a high, reliable sample. 65% gave the top rating (10/10); over 85% rated it 9 or 10. Over 99% agreed the content would help their professional work. The trainer's competence, clarity, and ability to create a safe space for questions were highlighted. The training's practical focus — concrete guidance on talking with a student in crisis, empathy, active listening, naming emotions, avoiding hasty advice — was most valued, along with clearer recognition of warning signs (including self-destructive behavior and suicidal ideation) and clear response frameworks/emotional-first-aid steps. Participants reported greater vigilance, less labeling of student behavior, more willingness to have difficult conversations (including about suicide), and stronger awareness of the limits of the teacher's role and the value of team collaboration/referral. Most found the agenda complete; the few suggestions concerned more practical examples/case studies, deeper coverage of talking with parents, and more attention to teacher well-being and post-death/grief procedures. Organizational notes (format, length, timing) were secondary. Conclusion: the training substantially increased teachers' and specialists' knowledge, competence and confidence in recognizing and supporting students in psychological crisis; continuing and expanding the program (especially modules on parent cooperation and staff well-being) is warranted.

PART II — "PARENTS OF A STUDENT IN PSYCHOLOGICAL CRISIS – HOW TO MOTIVATE THEM TO COOPERATE?"
====================================================================
This training addressed how teachers and school specialists cooperate with parents/guardians of a student in psychological crisis: why it's hard for parents to accept difficult information about their child, how to plan and conduct such a conversation, how to support and motivate parental cooperation, what recommendations a parent should leave the meeting with, and the broader (non-school) support system for youth psychological crises.

1,212 people participated (per-school breakdown included in the original, similar range as Part I).

--- Evaluation survey ---
Format: same structure (6 single-choice, 6 open, 3 scaled). 964 respondents (~79.5% return rate).

Q: Was the delivery format appropriate? Yes: 955 (99.1%) | No: 9 (0.9%)

159 people commented on possible changes. Key themes:
1. No comments / no need for change (dominant) — very positive.
2. Format, pace, monotony, length — e.g., monotone voice, too fast/dynamic delivery, sessions felt long/monotonous relative to content, technical voice issues, request for more concreteness in less time, fewer generalities, or conversely a wish for a longer session/more sessions.
3. Participant engagement / real-world examples — wanting more teacher sharing of experience and more real-case examples.
4. Organizational/technical format — preference for in-person; request to email slide summaries; ask participants to log in with full name; technical issues that blocked hearing the training.
5. Substantive/context notes — relevance given that many children don't tell parents about problems, teachers often learn first; frustration that institutional/bureaucratic systems ("urzędy") are slow even when schools do their part.

Open question — "Which content will be most useful? Why?" Key themes:
1. How to talk with a parent — stages, plan, structure of the conversation; participants valued a "ready instruction" / roadmap for such meetings, and the sense of security it gave them.
2. Parental resistance / lack of cooperation — recognizing it and responding: many wanted (and valued) tools for handling a "resistant parent," understanding that resistance often reflects grief, fear or overwhelm rather than hostility.
3. Ready-made phrasings/questions/examples for talking to parents about a difficult topic.
4. Frameworks from the training — the "4Z" rule, "3 principles of effective communication," meeting phases, stages of grief, phases of accepting a child's diagnosis.

6. Recognizing a student's crisis — symptoms, impact on school functioning, safety planning, risk after psychiatric discharge, not minimizing the child's emotions.
7. Supporting parents and encouraging them toward specialist help — how to persuade a parent to seek therapy/psychiatric care for the child; what to do when a parent denies the problem.
8. Formal/administrative/procedural/legal aspects; institutional referral pathways (e.g., PPP — psychological-pedagogical counseling centers).
9. School–parent relationship, boundaries, atmosphere; building rapport, setting boundaries respectfully.
10. Parent psychoeducation / recommended reading.
11. Other: reduced pressure from acknowledging that lack of parental engagement has many causes; courage to raise difficult conversations; not dismissing small concerns students bring up. A couple of critical/dismissive comments ("not relevant, I'm a subject teacher not a psychiatrist").

Open question — "Did the meeting change how you see certain issues? Which?" Key themes:
1. The parent's perspective/empathy/parental emotions/"grief" — understanding what parents go through, that a parent may be grieving the image they had of their child, needing patience as parents move through stages similar to grief.
2. Parental resistance/denial — better understanding its sources and mechanisms; realizing the teacher needn't carry the whole burden alone.
3. How to talk with parents — communication approach, wording, structure; reinforced understanding that the school supports but is not a therapy provider.
4. Parental reactions to receiving difficult news about their child.
5. Recognizing a student's crisis / not minimizing symptoms / acting sooner.
6. Teacher/school role vs. specialists/institutions and parental responsibility — the parent, not the teacher, bears primary responsibility; it's fine to refer onward without feeling like a failure.
7. Supporting and motivating parents toward cooperation/specialist help.
8. Boundaries, not taking a parent's defensive reaction personally — recognizing that a parent's pushback against the school isn't necessarily an attack.
9. Other: how to talk with parents of children with suicidal thoughts; not minimizing a child's emotions; being honest rather than falsely reassuring ("everything will be fine"). One critical response found the training added little of practical value given years of classroom experience.

Open question — "Anything unnecessary in the agenda?" Key themes:
1. No unnecessary content / everything needed and coherent (dominant).
2. Suggestions to shorten/compress — e.g., the first hour felt too long, some repetition (including repetition across sessions in the series), slides with many redundant example answers.
3. Content flagged as "unnecessary" or "mismatched" by a few — e.g., the "5 points on reactions to diagnosis," parts on resistance, communication rules (seen by a couple as already familiar).
4. Requests for more practice — more time on case analysis/practical exercises.
5. Other minor remarks (e.g., wanting a break).

Open question — "Was anything important missing?" Key themes:
1. Nothing missing / topic exhausted (dominant).

9. Other: one respondent found the training added nothing to their experience; most others found it thorough, interactive, and well-answered via Q&A.

--- Summary (Part II) ---
The training was rated very highly and met its substantive and organizational goals. Nearly all respondents found the format appropriate and the content clear, logically structured and practically oriented to real needs of teachers and specialists working with students and their families. Exercises and examples were valued for translating theory into concrete workplace actions; the trainer's competence, professionalism and openness were highlighted. The training substantially broadened/organized knowledge, especially regarding student crises, parental reaction mechanisms, and empathetic, well-planned communication. Many respondents reported a shift in how they view parental resistance — as a natural reaction rooted in fear, helplessness or a grief-like process rather than hostility — and increased confidence in conducting difficult conversations and understanding the limits of the school's role. Most found no missing or unnecessary content, judging the program coherent and comprehensive; occasional suggestions concerned more practice, case studies, longer duration, or an in-person format. Organizational feedback (timing, technical issues) did not diminish the very positive overall assessment. The training effectively strengthened participants' competence in working with students in crisis and building parent cooperation.

--- Summary (Part VIII) ---
Results clearly indicate a very high rating and strong educational/preventive value. Most respondents found the webinar substantive, practical, and responsive to real parental needs — both in broadening knowledge and in better understanding addiction mechanisms and the family's role in supporting a child. Participants said the session helped organize existing knowledge, understand warning signals, and gain concrete communication/intervention guidance. High scaled ratings confirm the content was seen as useful in daily interaction with a child and effective at increasing empathy and awareness of the challenges children and teenagers face. A very high share would recommend the session to other parents, reflecting strong satisfaction and trust in the presenter's quality and competence; participants also expressed strong belief in the value of the Wspierająca Szkoła program as a genuine preventive tool against risky and self-destructive youth behavior. Key strengths cited: high knowledge level, clear and accessible delivery, extensive practical references, presenter experience and professionalism, organized structure, concrete examples of actions and phrasing usable in a relationship with a child, and the opportunity to ask questions. The most frequently cited weakness was too-short duration relative to the breadth and importance of the topic; some also noted the lack of a recording or way to revisit content, which hindered note-taking and retention; a few mentioned pace, technical issues, and a lack of practical examples or supplementary reading/case materials. Notably, a large share of respondents reported no perceived weaknesses at all. Conclusion: results confirm very high substantive quality, appropriate topic selection, and the case for continuing and expanding this educational activity within Wspierająca Szkoła — feedback also points to demand for further sessions, ideally as a series, with access to recordings and deeper materials to strengthen the program's long-term educational and preventive impact.

CONCLUSIONS
====================================================================
Analysis of the evaluation surveys carried out under Edition II of the "Wspierająca Szkoła" program clearly shows that the program was an initiative of very high substantive, practical and social value. Teachers, school specialists, and parents/guardians of children and youth alike rated the project's activities as needed, well-designed, and genuinely supportive of them in their day-to-day educational and professional roles.

Across all respondent groups, a large majority reported that participating in the trainings and meetings let them gain new knowledge or organize/systematize knowledge they already had. High and very high ratings dominated the scaled questions on clarity of delivery, usefulness of content, trainer competence, and the meetings' impact on participants' sense of readiness to respond to psychological crises in children and youth. Negative or low ratings were sporadic and isolated, confirming the coherence and effectiveness of the program's overall concept.

A particularly important finding is that participants very highly valued the practical character of the content delivered. Both teachers and parents repeatedly emphasized the value of ready-made tools, response frameworks, sample phrases, and clearly defined steps for responding in crisis situations. Most frequently cited as useful were: talking with a child/student in crisis, the skill of asking the right questions, avoiding consolation and judgment, using empathy, recognizing warning signals, and knowing when to hand a case to another specialist or service.

The results also clearly show the program shifted how participants think about psychological crisis in children and youth. Respondents reported greater sensitivity to crisis signals, better understanding of problematic behavior as a possible "cry for help," and greater willingness to have difficult conversations — including about self-harm and suicidal thoughts. Many responses pointed to overcoming the fear of naming problems directly and moving away from a reflex to quickly "fix" the situation, in favor of attentive presence and listening.

From the schools' perspective, an especially important finding is that training participants reported a stronger sense that their institutions are now better prepared to respond to students' psychological crises. Respondents emphasized the value of clear procedures, teamwork, and awareness of the limits of their own competence. The "relay of help" motif — the ability to hand responsibility to other people or institutions — was repeatedly cited as reducing teachers' sense of overload and anxiety.

Analysis of open-ended answers on strengths and weaknesses shows a clear preponderance of positive assessments. Among strengths, opinions most often referenced trainers' high competence and practical experience, clear and understandable language, and the ability to combine theory with real-life and professional examples. Where weaknesses were noted, they mainly concerned organizational and technical matters (session length, online format, lack of recordings, fast pace) rather than substantive quality.

A recurring request was for continuation of these activities — further trainings, session series, deeper workshops, and materials allowing participants to revisit the content after webinars ended. Both teachers and parents expressed willingness to take part in further such initiatives, reflecting a high level of trust in the program and its organizers.

In summary, the results of all evaluation surveys consistently confirm that the "Wspierająca Szkoła" program effectively achieved its goals: it strengthened adults' competencies, increased their sense of agency and readiness to respond to psychological crises in children and youth, and met the real needs of both the school and family environment. The program can be considered a model example of prevention activity grounded in scientific knowledge, practical experience, and the real needs of its recipients, and provides a solid foundation for further development and nationwide dissemination of this type of initiative.

The "Wspierająca Szkoła" program is an important step toward strengthening psychological support in Polish schools. Through its continuation and the implementation of these recommendations, it can become an even more effective tool in counteracting psychological crises among children and youth.
